# Children and Family Practice Models

## Purpose

Children's social work uses legal frameworks, statutory guidance, local safeguarding procedures, professional judgement, family engagement, and practice models. A model can help structure the work, but it must not replace the child's lived experience, legal thresholds, supervision, or local policy.

Use this section to ask: which approach helps us understand the child's world, build safety, involve family networks, address harm, and test whether change is real?

## Practice Caution

Many models are implemented differently across local authorities and partner agencies. Some require specific training, licensing, tools, or supervision arrangements.

Before using a model in live practice, check:

- local procedure and authorised tools
- supervision and management expectations
- statutory guidance and legal thresholds
- the child's wishes, feelings, communication needs, identity, culture, and relationships
- consent, information-sharing, confidentiality, and safety
- whether the model fits the concern or is being forced onto the case

## Model and Hypothesis Map

### Signs of Safety

Signs of Safety is widely used in child protection practice to structure worries, strengths, danger statements, safety goals, scaling questions, safety planning, and family network work.

Core questions:

- What are we worried about?
- What is working well?
- What needs to happen?

Practice hypothesis:

- The family and professional network may be able to build a clear safety plan when worries are written plainly, existing safety is evidenced, and the family network understands what must change.

Questions to test:

- Is the danger statement clear enough for the family and child to understand?
- What existing safety has been demonstrated, not just promised?
- Who is in the safety network, and what will they actually do?

### Signs of Wellbeing, Success, and Belonging

These adaptations are often used around early help, family support, children in care, permanence, reunification, and belonging.

Practice hypothesis:

- The child may need a clearer picture of what wellbeing, stability, identity, family connection, and belonging would look like in daily life.

Questions to test:

- What does the child say or show about where they feel safe and connected?
- What would meaningful stability look like?
- How are culture, identity, relationships, and belonging being protected?

### Family Safeguarding

Family Safeguarding is a whole-family model often associated with bringing together children's social work and adult-facing help around domestic abuse, substance misuse, mental health, and parenting.

Practice hypothesis:

- Child safety may improve when adult needs and risks are addressed alongside direct child protection work.

Questions to test:

- Which adult needs are directly affecting child safety?
- Are adult services and children's services working to the same safety outcomes?
- How will adult-focused change be measured through the child's lived experience?

### Contextual Safeguarding

Contextual Safeguarding focuses on extra-familial harm: peer groups, schools, neighbourhoods, transport, online spaces, exploitation, and other contexts outside the family home.

Practice hypothesis:

- Harm may be located in contexts that parents cannot control alone, so intervention may need to target peer, school, community, or online settings.

Questions to test:

- Where is the harm happening?
- Who has power or influence in that context?
- What needs to change in the context, not only in the child or family?

### Family Group Conferencing

A Family Group Conference supports the wider family and trusted network to make a plan for the child, usually with private family time and professional safety checks.

Practice hypothesis:

- The wider family network may hold safe, practical solutions that professionals have not yet mapped or invited in.

Questions to test:

- Who does the child identify as family or trusted network?
- What information does the family need before planning?
- How will the plan be checked for safety, clarity, contingency, and review?

### Systemic Practice

Systemic practice looks at family patterns, roles, communication, meanings, repeated cycles, and how one person's change affects the wider network.

Practice hypothesis:

- The concern may be maintained by relationship patterns, family roles, professional responses, or repeated cycles.

Questions to test:

- What pattern keeps repeating?
- What does each person think the problem is?
- What would change if one part of the system responded differently?

### Motivational Interviewing

Motivational Interviewing can help with ambivalence, defensiveness, adolescent risk, substance use, parental engagement, and behaviour change conversations.

Practice hypothesis:

- The parent or young person may be caught between reasons to change and reasons to stay the same.

Questions to test:

- What does the person want to be different?
- What are their reasons for and against change?
- Are professionals arguing for change so strongly that the person is pushed into arguing against it?

### Solution-Focused Practice

Solution-focused practice explores goals, exceptions, scaling, strengths, and small signs of progress.

Practice hypothesis:

- Exceptions to the problem may reveal what can be repeated, strengthened, and built into the plan.

Questions to test:

- When is the problem slightly less serious?
- What would one point higher on the safety scale look like?
- What has the family already done that helped, even briefly?

### PACE and DDP-Informed Practice

PACE means Playfulness, Acceptance, Curiosity, and Empathy. It is often associated with trauma and attachment-informed work with children who have experienced relational harm, care disruption, shame, or mistrust.

Practice hypothesis:

- The child may need relational safety before they can reflect, trust, or change behaviour.

Questions to test:

- How might the behaviour make sense as communication or protection?
- How can adults stay curious without shaming or escalating?
- What helps the child experience safety in the relationship?

### Safe and Together

Safe and Together is a domestic abuse-informed child welfare model. It focuses on the perpetrator's pattern of behaviour, the impact on children, and the protective efforts of the non-abusive parent.

Practice hypothesis:

- Child safety work should focus on the behaviour of the person causing harm and avoid blaming the protective parent for the abuse.

Questions to test:

- What is the perpetrator's pattern of coercive control or harm?
- What has the protective parent done to promote safety?
- Does the plan increase accountability for the person causing harm?

### Graded Care Profile 2

Graded Care Profile 2 is a structured tool used in some areas to assess neglect by looking at domains of care and the child's experience over time.

Practice hypothesis:

- The quality of care may need observable, evidence-based mapping rather than vague statements about neglect.

Questions to test:

- Which care domains are strong, inconsistent, or harmful?
- What has been observed rather than assumed?
- Is change measurable over time?

### Restorative Practice

Restorative practice focuses on harm, accountability, repair, relationships, voice, and shared problem-solving.

Practice hypothesis:

- Progress may come through safe repair, accountability, and relationship work rather than professional instruction alone.

Questions to test:

- Who has been affected, and what do they need?
- What repair is realistic and safe?
- How will the process keep the child central and avoid blame?

## Recording Model-Informed Reasoning

Use this structure:

```text
Model or approach considered:
Why this model may fit:
Child's wishes, feelings, and lived experience:
Family and network view:
Evidence supporting this model:
Evidence challenging this model:
Legal threshold or statutory guidance engaged:
Safety, risk, and protective factors:
Local procedure or supervision advice:
Plan, contingency, and review:
```

## Common Pitfalls

- Using a model as a script rather than professional analysis.
- Treating parental engagement as evidence of child safety without checking impact.
- Focusing only on the home when harm is extra-familial.
- Focusing only on extra-familial risk and missing harm at home.
- Blaming a protective parent for domestic abuse caused by someone else.
- Naming strengths without testing whether they create actual safety.
- Missing the child's own words, behaviour, communication, identity, and relationships.
- Forgetting legal thresholds, statutory guidance, local procedure, and supervision.
