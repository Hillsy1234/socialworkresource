# Safeguarding Adults in Wales

## Learning outcomes

Recognise the Welsh adult-at-risk framework, distinguish reporting from enquiries, and produce a clear record of concerns, participation, immediate action and follow-up.

## Three questions about an adult at risk

Section 126(1) concerns an adult experiencing or at risk of abuse or neglect, with care and support needs, who is unable to protect themselves from that abuse or neglect because of those needs. The needs do not have to be met by the authority already. Risk can justify attention before actual harm has occurred. Read the [Wales Safeguarding Procedures](https://safeguarding.wales/en/adu-i/adu-i-a2/a2-p2/).

## Reporting and enquiries are different responsibilities

The local authority's section 126(2) enquiry duty arises where it has reasonable cause to suspect an adult in its area is an adult at risk. Section 128 sets reporting duties for relevant partners. Record your role and the applicable duty rather than treating every practitioner as the authority making the enquiry decision. Refer to the [adult enquiry procedures](https://www.safeguarding.wales/en/adu-i/adu-i-a3pt1/) and [Volume 6 statutory guidance](https://www.gov.wales/sites/default/files/publications/2019-06/volume-6-handling-individual-cases-to-protect-adults-at-risk.pdf).

## A practical sequence

1. Address immediate danger and urgent health needs through the appropriate emergency service. Do not wait for a completed learning checklist.
2. Record the concern, source, time and observable impact. Separate direct observation, what someone said and your interpretation.
3. Speak with the adult in a way that supports safety and communication. Explore what they want, who they trust, and any pressure affecting their choices.
4. Follow the local reporting route, share relevant information securely, and confirm who received it. Keep an escalation route where a response is missing or the concern increases.
5. Contribute to agreed enquiries and protection planning within your role. Record the rationale, responsibilities, review and the adult's involvement.

## Consent, capacity and coercion

A wish not to involve services needs careful exploration and recording. It does not automatically settle whether information can or must be shared. Identify relevant duties, risks to others, confidentiality and data-protection requirements, with advice where needed. A capacity assessment answers a specific decision; it is not a blanket assessment of whether someone can “safeguard themselves”. Coercion and barriers to speaking freely also need attention.

## Self-neglect and organisational concerns

Investigate context: pain, loss, poverty, isolation, environmental barriers and previous experiences of services can matter. Do not make a threshold decision from a label alone. For concerns in an organisation, examine patterns across records, staffing, communication and other people potentially affected. A single service response may not address a wider pattern. Follow employer and regional procedures concerning practitioners or people in positions of trust.

## Worked learning example

Fictional example: Rhys needs help with meals and finances. A relative has moved in; Rhys appears anxious and has missed meals. Record what is known without assuming the relative is responsible. Arrange a safe opportunity for Rhys to speak, clarify immediate needs and consider the statutory elements. A report should explain why further enquiries may be needed, what Rhys wants and what has already been done. It should not claim abuse is proved when it is suspected.

## Recording check

Use the safeguarding prompt to document the three statutory elements, evidence and gaps, the adult's wishes, communication/advocacy needs, reporting duty considered, information shared, interim safety action and the responsible contact. Keep allegations and findings distinct. Revisit the plan when new facts or wishes emerge.
