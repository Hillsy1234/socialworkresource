01 - Professional Foundations

Purpose

This module builds the habits that sit underneath every legal framework: rights, evidence, relationships, professional curiosity, ethical judgement, and clear recording.

Core Social Work Tasks

Social workers are often doing several tasks at once:

  • hearing the person's story and wishes
  • understanding risk without reducing the person to risk
  • promoting rights, autonomy, family life, dignity, culture, and identity
  • assessing needs and eligibility
  • coordinating multi-agency responses
  • challenging oppressive or unsafe systems
  • making defensible decisions under law and policy
  • recording clearly enough that another person can understand what happened and why

Social Work England Professional Standards

The six standards can be used as a supervision framework:

  1. Promote the rights, strengths, and wellbeing of people, families, and communities.
  2. Establish and maintain trust and confidence.
  3. Be accountable for the quality of practice and decisions.
  4. Maintain continuing professional development.
  5. Act safely, respectfully, and with professional integrity.
  6. Promote ethical practice and report concerns.

Strengths-Based Practice

Strengths-based work asks:

  • What matters to this person?
  • What is already working?
  • Who and what helps?
  • What has the person survived or managed before?
  • What community, cultural, spiritual, family, or peer resources are meaningful?
  • What would "better" look like in the person's own words?

Avoid turning strengths-based practice into optimism without evidence. It is not "everything is fine"; it is "the person has agency, context, relationships, and resources as well as needs and risks."

Professional Curiosity

Professional curiosity means noticing when the account, presentation, environment, or records do not fit together.

Use it when:

  • there are repeated crises with unclear causes
  • family or professionals speak over the person
  • injuries, money problems, missed appointments, or self-neglect patterns are explained away
  • the person seems compliant but frightened, sedated, confused, or resigned
  • care arrangements look restrictive but are described as "just normal care"
  • risk is minimised because services are under pressure

Trauma-Informed Practice

Assume that distress, avoidance, anger, dissociation, substance use, withdrawal, and mistrust may be survival responses.

Practise:

  • safety before disclosure
  • choice wherever possible
  • collaboration rather than doing to
  • clarity about your role and powers
  • predictable communication
  • consent and control over information sharing where possible
  • attention to shame, fear, culture, oppression, and previous service harm

Evidence and Analysis

Separate these four things:

Fact: What is known and how do we know it?
Account: What has someone said, and who said it?
Analysis: What does it mean?
Decision: What are we doing, under what power or duty, and why?

Weak recording:

Mary lacks capacity and is unsafe at home.

Stronger recording:

Mary was unable to explain what would happen if she left the gas hob on overnight, despite information being repeated in short sentences and supported with pictures. She said "the house sorts itself out". This relates to the decision about whether she can safely prepare hot food without support today. I assessed that she could not understand or use/weigh the relevant risk information for this decision at this time. A best interests decision is required about meal preparation support.

Supervision Questions

  • What is the legal basis for our involvement?
  • What does the person say they want?
  • What are the strongest arguments for doing less?
  • What are the strongest arguments for doing more?
  • What evidence would change our view?
  • Where are we relying on family or provider accounts without independent observation?
  • What is the plan if the risk increases tonight?
  • Is this a disagreement, an executive functioning issue, coercion, incapacity, mental disorder, or unmet need?

Common Pitfalls

  • Treating risk as proof of incapacity.
  • Treating compliance as consent.
  • Treating family disagreement as safeguarding without clarifying abuse or neglect.
  • Treating a service shortage as evidence that no alternative exists.
  • Writing conclusions without explaining the evidence.
  • Forgetting carers, children, housing, finances, culture, immigration, domestic abuse, and substance use.
  • Using the least restrictive phrase without showing the options considered.

Learning Task

Take a case you know well and write a 10-line analysis:

  1. What the person wants.
  2. Main strengths.
  3. Main needs.
  4. Main risks.
  5. Capacity/consent issues.
  6. Legal duties or powers.
  7. Options considered.
  8. Least restrictive plan.
  9. Contingency.
  10. Review date.
