05 - Mental Health Act

Purpose

The Mental Health Act 1983, as amended, provides powers for compulsory assessment, detention, treatment, guardianship, community treatment, and forensic mental health pathways in England and Wales.

The Mental Health Act 2025 received Royal Assent on 18 December 2025 and reforms the 1983 Act. Implementation is phased. Section 56(1) commenced sections 30(2), 32, 35, 36(1) and (3)(b), 38 and 39 on 18 February 2026. Sections 51 and 52 followed on 6 April 2026; sections 53–57 commenced at Royal Assent. This does not mean the wider reforms are all operational. Always check commencement, the current Code of Practice, local AMHP guidance, and legal advice.

Core Principles in Practice

Use Mental Health Act powers only when the legal criteria are met and less restrictive alternatives are not sufficient.

AMHP analysis should include:

  • nature and degree of mental disorder
  • risk to the person or others
  • available informal and community alternatives
  • whether admission is necessary
  • whether detention is necessary
  • person's wishes and feelings
  • nearest relative or nominated person position, depending on provisions in force
  • equality and cultural issues
  • trauma and coercion risks
  • human rights
  • aftercare and discharge planning

Common Civil Sections

Section 2

Admission for assessment, or assessment followed by treatment. Usually up to 28 days.

Use where:

  • assessment is needed
  • detention is necessary for health/safety or protection of others
  • the situation cannot safely be managed informally

Section 3

Admission for treatment. Usually up to 6 months initially.

Use where:

  • mental disorder is of a nature or degree making hospital treatment appropriate
  • treatment is available
  • detention is necessary for health/safety or protection of others
  • appropriate treatment test is met

Section 4

Emergency admission for assessment, based on one medical recommendation. Use only when urgent and waiting for a second doctor would cause undesirable delay.

Section 5

Holding powers for informal inpatients already in hospital:

  • Section 5(2): doctor/approved clinician holding power.
  • Section 5(4): nurse holding power.

These are not community powers and do not authorise treatment in themselves.

Sections 135 and 136

Police powers connected to places of safety:

  • Section 135 requires a warrant to enter premises.
  • Section 136 is not limited to public places. Subject to its other criteria, it applies outside the private residential premises excluded by section 136(1A), including the specified associated premises. Check the exact statutory exclusions rather than assuming all privately owned places are excluded. Section 136.

Always consider trauma, proportionality, health-based places of safety, and least restrictive conveyance.

Guardianship

Guardianship can require a person to live at a specified place, attend for treatment/occupation/training/education, and allow access by professionals. It is less restrictive than detention but still a legal power.

Community Treatment Orders

CTOs apply after certain detained treatment sections and allow discharge with conditions and recall powers. Consider proportionality, therapeutic purpose, and whether conditions are realistic.

AMHP Role

The AMHP is not a rubber stamp. The AMHP makes an independent application decision.

Key AMHP tasks:

  • coordinate assessment
  • interview the person in a suitable manner
  • consult nearest relative/nominated person where required
  • consult carers and involved professionals where appropriate
  • consider social circumstances
  • explore alternatives to detention
  • consider children, dependants, pets, housing, finances, and immediate practical needs
  • arrange conveyance if application is made
  • record reasons clearly

Mental Health Act 2025 Reform Watch

Targeted commencement update: 22 October 2026. Establish whether each provision is legally in force, then check applicable guidance, forms and local implementation. Local policy does not determine when legislation commences.

Commenced supervised-discharge provisions

The February changes include a route for relevant restricted patients to be conditionally discharged with deprivation-of-liberty conditions where the statutory criteria are satisfied. HMPPS guidance explains applications through the Secretary of State for Justice. This is a distinct MHA route; do not treat every community restriction as a DoLS or Court of Protection case. Obtain specialist advice on eligibility, conditions and safeguards.

Sources: Section 56 commencement; HMPPS supervised discharge guidance.

The 2025 Act aims to:

  • strengthen patient voice and involvement
  • give statutory weight to care and treatment planning
  • increase scrutiny of detention
  • reduce unnecessary detention
  • address racial disparities
  • strengthen involvement of families and carers
  • change nearest relative arrangements toward nominated person arrangements when commenced
  • limit use of detention for autistic people and people with learning disabilities without co-occurring mental disorder when relevant provisions commence
  • improve provisions around justice settings and places of safety

Because implementation is phased, practitioners should ask:

Is this provision in force today?
Has the Code of Practice been updated?
What does local AMHP policy say?
What training or forms have changed?
Does this affect nearest relative/nominated person, care planning, tribunal, CTO, or learning disability/autism criteria?

MHA and MCA Interface

Use the Mental Health Act when detention for assessment or treatment of mental disorder may be required and criteria are met.

Use MCA for decision-specific capacity and best interests matters outside MHA treatment powers, including:

  • physical healthcare
  • accommodation decisions after discharge
  • finances
  • contact
  • care planning
  • advance decisions outside overridden MHA treatment provisions

Ordinary MCA care and treatment powers do not themselves authorise deprivation of liberty. Where the person lacks capacity, a separate DoLS or Court of Protection authorisation may be available, subject to section 4A and Schedule 1A eligibility. This can include psychiatric care for a non-objecting person when the statutory criteria are met; non-objection alone does not establish eligibility. Section 28 limits MCA authority for treatment regulated by Part IV of the MHA. DoLS authorises deprivation of liberty, not treatment itself. Consider an MHA assessment where appropriate and obtain specialist advice on the actual admission, treatment, objections and legal status.

Sources: MCA sections 4A and 28 and Schedules A1/1A. Check commencement annotations: prospective amendments are not necessarily operative.

MHA and DoLS Interface

DoLS may apply to hospital or care home arrangements for a person lacking capacity where deprivation of liberty is identified.

The MHA may be required instead where:

  • the person objects to mental health admission or treatment
  • detention is needed for treatment of mental disorder
  • MHA criteria are met
  • DoLS eligibility is excluded

This is a high-risk interface. Seek AMHP/legal advice.

MHA and Care Act Interface

A person subject to MHA may still need:

  • Care Act assessment
  • carers assessment
  • safeguarding enquiry
  • accommodation planning
  • ordinary residence analysis
  • Section 117 aftercare if eligible
  • advocacy
  • transition planning

Section 117 Aftercare

Section 117 aftercare may apply after detention under certain sections, including Section 3. It is a joint duty on health and local authority bodies to provide aftercare services meeting needs arising from or related to the person's mental disorder and reducing risk of readmission.

Do not confuse Section 117 aftercare with ordinary Care Act charging arrangements.

Common Pitfalls

  • Treating diagnosis as automatic need for detention.
  • Treating risk alone as enough without legal criteria.
  • Missing community alternatives.
  • Not exploring informal admission.
  • Forgetting the person's children or caring roles.
  • Not recording why less restrictive options were insufficient.
  • Using MCA/DoLS to avoid MHA where the person is objecting to mental health treatment.
  • Ignoring racial disparity, cultural context, and mistrust of services.
