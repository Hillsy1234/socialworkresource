Professional Foundations — New South Wales

The Australian Association of Social Workers (AASW) Code of Ethics and Practice Standards provide a professional framework. AASW membership or accreditation, an employer requirement and statutory authority are different matters. Check the requirements for the actual role and state rather than presenting the professional association as the source of every legal power.

Practice should attend to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples’ self-determination, cultural safety and the effects of colonisation. Ask which community-controlled organisations or cultural supports the person wants involved. Professional values need to be connected to the state law governing the particular intervention.

  • Identify the employer, state, service setting and any appointment required for the task.
  • Explain the professional role, consent and confidentiality limits before assessment.
  • Consider power, racism, disability and material barriers alongside individual and family strengths.
  • Use supervision and learning to test the effect of practice on the person, with the current AASW standards as a professional reference.

Distinguish an employer complaint, a professional-association concern and a statutory regulator or tribunal route. The relevant health, disability, aged-care or child-protection body depends on the issue. A professional membership complaint is not an appeal against a state or Commonwealth funding decision.

  • AASW Code of Ethics

Social Care and Support — New South Wales

Disability support through the NDIS, state health and housing services, and Commonwealth aged care have separate entry and funding rules. NDIS access involves age, residence and disability or early-intervention requirements; a diagnosis alone does not establish entitlement to a particular funded support. Check current NDIA rules for the actual application or plan.

The Aged Care Act 2024 and Support at Home commenced on 1 November 2025. Do not describe the old Home Care Package arrangements as the only current system. The Commonwealth Home Support Programme has its own transition arrangements. Contributions and programme rules need a current check, including announced changes with later commencement dates.

  • Identify the scheme responsible for each need and the evidence required for its assessment.
  • Keep access eligibility, plan funding, provider availability and any client contribution separate.
  • Consider state health, housing and community support where a need is outside a particular funded scheme.
  • Record the person’s goals, family willingness, actual start dates and the review route for each decision.

For an NDIA decision, identify whether an internal review and subsequent Administrative Review Tribunal route are available and check the decision notice’s time limits. A provider complaint to the NDIS Commission is different from a funding review. Aged-care complaints and assessment reviews use their own routes.

  • NDIS legislative changes
  • Current aged-care provider rules and reforms
  • CHSP transition arrangements

Decision-Making and Capacity — New South Wales

The Guardianship Act 1987 provides important guardianship and medical-consent routes. For a patient unable to consent to relevant medical or dental treatment, identify the statutory person responsible: an authorised guardian first, then an eligible spouse or partner, unpaid carer, or close relative or friend in the prescribed order. Next of kin is not automatically the correct person.

Treatment categories and objections matter. NCAT guidance distinguishes ordinary substitute-consent situations from special treatment and situations involving an objection. An enduring guardian’s welfare functions and an attorney’s financial functions also require separate document checks.

  • Support the patient’s own decision and establish the precise treatment and capacity question.
  • Read the appointment and apply the person-responsible hierarchy, including eligibility and availability.
  • Check the treatment category and whether the patient objects; do not assume a representative can override that objection.
  • Seek the applicable NCAT or clinical/legal route where consent cannot lawfully be obtained through the ordinary hierarchy.

NCAT’s Guardianship Division can deal with relevant appointments, reviews and treatment-consent applications. Explain the person’s participation and access to advice. A guardianship order must be read for its specific functions; it is not a general transfer of all decisions.

  • NCAT person responsible guidance
  • Guardianship Act 1987
  • NCAT guardianship jurisdiction

Restrictions and Liberty — New South Wales

For NDIS services, the Commonwealth behaviour-support and restrictive-practices rules operate alongside NSW authorisation requirements. A behaviour support plan, the state authorisation process and provider reporting obligations are separate parts of the framework. Lodging a plan with the NDIS Commission is not itself NSW authorisation.

NSW DCJ publishes the Restrictive Practices Authorisation policy and procedural guide. Healthcare, aged care and mental-health settings have different rules. A person-responsible medical-consent role does not automatically authorise environmental restraint or confinement in a disability service.

  • Identify the practice, its purpose and whether it is a regulated restrictive practice in the actual setting.
  • Check the NSW authorisation process alongside the NDIS plan, provider and reporting requirements.
  • Verify consent or representative authority where relevant without treating it as a substitute for authorisation.
  • Record alternatives, conditions, monitoring, review and the strategy for reducing the practice.

The person should have accessible information, support to express objections and the applicable provider, NDIS Commission or legal review route. Unauthorised use may trigger incident-reporting duties. Check current rules for timing and recipients rather than waiting for routine plan review.

  • NSW restrictive-practices authorisation policy
  • NDIS implementing-provider rules

Mental Health — New South Wales

The Mental Health Act 2007 distinguishes mentally ill and mentally disordered persons and provides different assessment, detention and treatment routes. For the mentally ill person definition, mental illness must connect to the need for care, treatment or control to protect the person or others from serious harm. The mentally disordered route has its own serious-physical-harm test and limits.

Check the Act’s less restrictive care requirements and the exact role of authorised medical officers, the Mental Health Review Tribunal and other authorised persons. Community treatment orders and inpatient detention are different measures. A social-work qualification alone does not confer every transport or detention power.

  • Identify the proposed route and connect the actual evidence to its criteria.
  • Consider whether appropriate care is reasonably available through a less restrictive alternative.
  • Verify the assessment, certification, notice and tribunal requirements for that status.
  • Separate psychiatric treatment powers from consent to unrelated medical treatment and from the discharge support plan.

Explain rights information, the applicable carer or designated-person arrangements, legal representation and Mental Health Review Tribunal review. Read the person’s actual documents and the current statutory time limits rather than treating every admission as a fixed period that must be completed.

  • Mental Health Act 2007, current text
  • Mental Health Act 2007, current PDF

Adult Safeguarding — New South Wales

The Ageing and Disability Commissioner Act 2019 and the NSW Ageing and Disability Commission (ADC) provide a route for concerns about abuse, neglect or exploitation of older people and adults with disability. The ADC considers reports, advice, referrals and investigation within its remit, with consent and statutory exceptions relevant to investigation.

Concerns about an NDIS provider, an aged-care service, a health service, crime or family violence may require a different or additional route. Identify the setting and relationship; one report to the ADC does not necessarily discharge another organisation’s incident-reporting duties.

  • Arrange a safe conversation and identify immediate danger, dependency and the adult’s desired outcomes.
  • Consider an ADC report where there are reasonable grounds for the relevant concern.
  • Identify any police, NDIS Commission, aged-care or provider reporting requirement separately.
  • Record consent and information-sharing reasoning, practical safety support and who will follow up.

The ADC can explain its response and refer matters beyond its scope. Support the adult to use the relevant complaint or legal route and preserve participation where they fear retaliation. A referral is not permission to impose a placement or take over finances.

  • ADC response to reports
  • Ageing and Disability Commissioner Act 2019

Children, Families and Transitions — New South Wales

The Children and Young Persons (Care and Protection) Act 1998 provides the child-protection framework. Mandatory-reporting obligations depend on the professional role and circumstances, including reasonable grounds to suspect a child is at risk of significant harm. The statutory mandatory duty for a child and the ability to report concerns about a young person must be distinguished.

DCJ’s Mandatory Reporter Guide supports decisions about the response but does not replace the Act or immediate safety action. Child-protection reporting, police reporting and an organisation’s incident or reportable-conduct procedures can be separate duties.

  • Check the child’s age, your role and the applicable reporting threshold under the Act.
  • Use the current DCJ guidance and appropriate Child Protection Helpline or other reporting route.
  • Record the concern, evidence, child’s account and report without waiting to prove the allegation.
  • Plan family support and cultural participation, including relevant Aboriginal placement and participation requirements where engaged.

Explain the role of DCJ, any court process and how the child and family can obtain support or legal advice. A voluntary support referral does not replace a mandatory report when its conditions are met. Preserve safe communication and reporter confidentiality under the applicable rules.

  • DCJ mandatory reporters
  • Children and Young Persons (Care and Protection) Act 1998

Rights, Culture and Recording — New South Wales

The Anti-Discrimination Act 1977 and relevant Commonwealth discrimination laws form part of the NSW rights map. For records, distinguish the Privacy and Personal Information Protection Act 1998 from the Health Records and Information Privacy Act 2002 (HRIP), as well as any Commonwealth privacy obligations applying to the organisation.

The HRIP health-privacy principles concern collection, storage, access, use and disclosure within their statutory scope. Identify whether the organisation is a public agency, health-service provider or another covered entity. A service’s privacy policy should explain its duties, not be treated as a separate permission to disregard them.

  • Identify the state or Commonwealth duty relevant to access, discrimination or the proposed intervention.
  • Arrange accessible communication and culturally safe participation chosen with the person.
  • Check the applicable information regime and lawful purpose before collection or disclosure.
  • Explain access, correction and complaint rights, including the appropriate Information and Privacy Commission route.

NSW IPC guidance distinguishes consent and other legal bases for information handling. A broad consent form does not remove all privacy obligations. A discrimination complaint, a privacy complaint and review of a service decision require different pathways.

  • HRIP Act and scope
  • Anti-Discrimination Act 1977
