02 - Care Act 2014

Purpose

The Care Act 2014 is the central adult social care framework in England. It creates duties around wellbeing, prevention, information and advice, assessment, eligibility, care and support planning, carers, safeguarding, market shaping, advocacy, continuity of care, and ordinary residence.

Core Principle: Wellbeing

The local authority must promote individual wellbeing when carrying out care and support functions. Wellbeing is broad and includes:

  • personal dignity
  • physical and mental health
  • emotional wellbeing
  • protection from abuse and neglect
  • control over day-to-day life
  • participation in work, education, training, or recreation
  • social and economic wellbeing
  • domestic, family, and personal relationships
  • suitability of living accommodation
  • contribution to society

Prevention

Think in three layers:

  1. Prevent needs from developing.
  2. Reduce needs that already exist.
  3. Delay deterioration or escalation.

Prevention is not a side issue. It is part of good assessment and planning.

Information and Advice

The local authority must establish and maintain accessible information and advice about care and support. This applies to the whole population, not only people already eligible for services.

Include information about:

  • how the care system works
  • local services and support
  • prevention
  • finances and charging
  • safeguarding
  • housing
  • support for carers
  • advocacy and complaints

Assessment Duty

An adult should receive a needs assessment where they appear to have needs for care and support. The threshold for assessment is appearance of need, not proof of eligibility.

Assessment should be:

  • person-centred
  • proportionate
  • strengths-based
  • outcome-focused
  • accessible
  • collaborative
  • evidence-led
  • mindful of fluctuating needs
  • joined up with carers, health, housing, and other services where relevant

Eligibility

Eligibility usually asks:

  1. Are the adult's needs caused by or related to a physical or mental impairment or illness?
  2. As a result, is the adult unable to achieve two or more specified outcomes?
  3. Is there, or is there likely to be, a significant impact on wellbeing?

Relevant outcomes include:

  • managing and maintaining nutrition
  • maintaining personal hygiene
  • managing toilet needs
  • being appropriately clothed
  • making use of the home safely
  • maintaining a habitable home environment
  • developing and maintaining family or other personal relationships
  • accessing work, training, education, or volunteering
  • making use of community facilities or services
  • carrying out caring responsibilities for a child

"Unable to Achieve" Can Mean

The person:

  • cannot achieve the outcome without assistance
  • can achieve it without assistance but doing so causes significant pain, distress, or anxiety
  • can achieve it without assistance but doing so endangers, or is likely to endanger, their health or safety or that of another person
  • can achieve it without assistance but doing so takes significantly longer than would normally be expected

Independent Advocacy

Consider Care Act advocacy where the person would experience substantial difficulty in:

  • understanding relevant information
  • retaining information
  • using or weighing information
  • communicating views, wishes, or feelings

and there is no appropriate person to support their involvement.

This is different from IMCA under the Mental Capacity Act, though both may be relevant in different situations.

Carers

A carer may need a carer's assessment where it appears they may have support needs. Do not wait for breakdown.

Ask:

  • What caring tasks are being provided?
  • Is the carer willing and able to continue?
  • What is the impact on their health, work, relationships, finances, sleep, identity, and safety?
  • Are they providing care because services are absent?
  • Is there risk of coercion, resentment, hidden harm, or neglect?
  • Does the cared-for person consent to information sharing, and how will confidentiality be handled?

Care and Support Planning

A good plan states:

  • eligible needs
  • personal outcomes
  • what will meet each need
  • who is responsible
  • personal budget
  • direct payment position if relevant
  • contingency
  • risk management
  • review date
  • how the person was involved

Review

Review when:

  • needs change
  • outcomes are not being met
  • risks increase
  • carer situation changes
  • hospital admission/discharge occurs
  • safeguarding concerns arise
  • the person requests review
  • the plan appears overly restrictive or insufficient

Ordinary Residence and Moving Areas

Ordinary residence affects which local authority is responsible. Be alert when:

  • a person moves between local authority areas
  • the move is arranged by a local authority
  • there are disputes between councils
  • the person lacks capacity and placement decisions are made in best interests
  • hospital discharge, supported living, or care home moves are involved

Do not let ordinary residence disputes delay urgent care and safety planning.

Care Act Assessment Structure

1. Reason for assessment
2. Person's own words and outcomes
3. Communication and accessibility needs
4. Relevant history and current situation
5. Strengths, informal support, community links
6. Daily living domains
7. Carer position
8. Mental capacity and consent issues
9. Risks and safeguarding
10. Preventive options
11. Eligibility analysis
12. Support plan and personal budget
13. Contingency and review

Care Act and MCA Interface

The Care Act tells you whether the person has care and support needs and what duties may follow.

The MCA tells you whether the person can make a specific decision, and what to do if they cannot.

Do not use a Care Act assessment to shortcut a capacity assessment. Do not use a capacity assessment to avoid Care Act duties.

Practice Prompts

  • What matters to the person, not just what is the matter with them?
  • What outcomes are currently blocked?
  • What would happen without support?
  • What support is already being provided, and is it sustainable?
  • What is the least formal response that would meet the need?
  • What is the evidence for eligibility?
  • What is the evidence for the plan being sufficient?

Common Pitfalls

  • Refusing assessment because the person may not be eligible.
  • Ignoring fluctuating needs because the person appeared well on one visit.
  • Treating family support as automatically available.
  • Missing carers assessments.
  • Treating information and advice as a substitute for meeting eligible needs.
  • Failing to record how wellbeing is affected.
  • Not considering advocacy early enough.
