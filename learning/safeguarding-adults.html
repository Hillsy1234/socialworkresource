06 - Safeguarding Adults

Purpose

Adult safeguarding protects an adult's right to live in safety, free from abuse and neglect. Under the Care Act 2014, safeguarding must be person-led, outcome-focused, proportionate, and rights-based.

Section 42 Duty

A local authority must make, or cause to be made, whatever enquiries it thinks necessary where it has reasonable cause to suspect that an adult:

  1. has needs for care and support, whether or not the authority is meeting those needs
  2. is experiencing, or is at risk of, abuse or neglect
  3. as a result of those needs is unable to protect themselves from the abuse, neglect, or risk

The threshold is reasonable cause to suspect, not proof.

Six Safeguarding Principles

  1. Empowerment - people are supported and encouraged to make their own decisions.
  2. Prevention - act before harm occurs where possible.
  3. Proportionality - use the least intrusive response appropriate to risk.
  4. Protection - support and representation for those in greatest need.
  5. Partnership - local solutions through services working with communities.
  6. Accountability - transparency and accountability in safeguarding practice.

Making Safeguarding Personal

Ask early:

  • What do you want to happen?
  • What are you worried about?
  • What would help you feel safer?
  • What do you not want us to do?
  • Who do you trust?
  • What would make things worse?
  • How should we communicate with you?

Making Safeguarding Personal does not mean doing only what the adult asks in every situation. It means the adult's outcomes, rights, and voice shape the response unless there is a lawful reason to override consent or act to protect others.

Types of Abuse and Neglect

Consider:

  • physical abuse
  • domestic abuse
  • sexual abuse
  • psychological or emotional abuse
  • financial or material abuse
  • modern slavery
  • discriminatory abuse
  • organisational abuse
  • neglect and acts of omission
  • self-neglect

Also consider exploitation, coercive control, cuckooing, mate crime, hoarding, fire risk, forced marriage, honour-based abuse, online abuse, and radicalisation.

Safeguarding Workflow

1. Concern identified
2. Immediate safety and urgent medical/police action if needed
3. Speak with the adult where safe and possible
4. Consider consent, capacity, coercion, and public interest
5. Decide whether Section 42 duty is met
6. Plan enquiry scope and who is best placed to enquire
7. Gather evidence and views
8. Analyse risk, strengths, protective factors, and desired outcomes
9. Create protection plan if needed
10. Review outcomes and close or continue

Consent and Information Sharing

Adults with capacity can usually make decisions others consider risky. But you may share information or act without consent where:

  • the person lacks capacity for the relevant decision and action is in best interests
  • others are at risk
  • a child is at risk
  • a serious crime may have been committed
  • coercion or undue influence may be affecting consent
  • vital interests are at stake
  • public interest or legal duties justify disclosure

Record the legal and ethical basis for information sharing.

Capacity in Safeguarding

Capacity may be needed for decisions such as:

  • whether to accept care
  • whether to remain at home
  • whether to have contact with a person alleged to cause harm
  • whether to manage money
  • whether to consent to information sharing
  • whether to engage with police

Do not ask "does the person have capacity for safeguarding?" Ask what specific decision is needed.

Self-Neglect

Self-neglect may include:

  • severe neglect of personal hygiene
  • malnutrition or dehydration
  • unsafe home conditions
  • hoarding
  • refusal of essential care or treatment
  • fire risk
  • repeated hospital admissions linked to living conditions

Key questions:

  • What does the person understand about the risks?
  • Is there executive dysfunction?
  • Is there trauma, depression, psychosis, dementia, substance use, coercion, poverty, grief, or isolation?
  • What matters to the person about their home and routines?
  • What small changes would they accept?
  • Are legal powers needed and proportionate?

Domestic Abuse

Domestic abuse may involve physical, emotional, sexual, economic, coercive, or controlling behaviour. Children who see, hear, or experience the effects of domestic abuse are recognised as victims in their own right under the Domestic Abuse Act 2021.

Practice points:

  • speak to the adult alone where safe
  • do not use family members as interpreters
  • assess immediate risk and safety planning
  • consider MARAC referral where high risk
  • consider children and adult safeguarding in parallel
  • consider housing, finances, immigration, pets, technology abuse, and stalking
  • avoid victim-blaming language

Organisational Abuse

Look for patterns:

  • repeated falls, pressure damage, medication errors, missed calls
  • high staff turnover
  • blanket restrictions
  • poor record keeping
  • closed cultures
  • people afraid to complain
  • restraint or sedation used for convenience
  • lack of personalised care

Consider provider action, commissioning, CQC, contracts, police, safeguarding enquiry, and individual protection plans.

Common Pitfalls

  • Waiting for proof before starting enquiries.
  • Ignoring the adult's desired outcomes.
  • Treating refusal as closure without analysing coercion, capacity, or public interest.
  • Treating self-neglect only as lifestyle choice.
  • Missing carers as people who may both need support and pose/experience risk.
  • Closing without reviewing whether outcomes improved.
  • Not linking safeguarding with Care Act assessment and care planning.
