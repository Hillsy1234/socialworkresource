08 - Equality, Human Rights, and Recording

Purpose

This module helps keep social work lawful, anti-oppressive, and defensible.

Equality Act 2010

Protected characteristics include:

  • age
  • disability
  • gender reassignment
  • marriage and civil partnership
  • pregnancy and maternity
  • race
  • religion or belief
  • sex
  • sexual orientation

Practice duties include:

  • making reasonable adjustments
  • avoiding direct and indirect discrimination
  • recognising harassment and victimisation
  • considering the Public Sector Equality Duty where relevant

Reasonable Adjustments

Adjustments may include:

  • accessible formats
  • interpreter
  • communication aids
  • extra time
  • quiet environment
  • home visit
  • involving a trusted person
  • flexible appointments
  • advocacy
  • sensory adaptations
  • accessible transport

Record adjustments offered and whether they worked.

Human Rights Act

Commonly relevant rights:

  • Article 2: right to life
  • Article 3: freedom from torture, inhuman or degrading treatment
  • Article 5: liberty and security
  • Article 6: fair process
  • Article 8: private and family life, home, and correspondence
  • Article 14: non-discrimination in enjoyment of rights

Human rights analysis is not abstract. It helps answer:

  • What right is engaged?
  • Is interference lawful?
  • Is there a legitimate aim?
  • Is the action necessary and proportionate?
  • Is there a less restrictive alternative?
  • Has the person been involved?
  • Are safeguards available?

Anti-Oppressive Practice

Ask:

  • How might racism, ableism, sexism, homophobia, transphobia, ageism, poverty, immigration status, trauma, or institutional mistrust shape this situation?
  • Whose account is being believed most easily?
  • Is the person being described in loaded language?
  • Are services expecting the person to adapt to the system rather than adapting the system to the person?
  • Is risk being overestimated because of stigma or underestimated because of familiarity?

Culturally Responsive Practice

Do:

  • ask, do not assume
  • use interpreters
  • recognise religious and cultural networks as possible strengths
  • explore family roles without stereotyping
  • understand migration, racism, asylum, and trauma contexts
  • consider food, prayer, gender, modesty, grief, and end-of-life customs
  • challenge harmful practice without condemning identity

Good Recording

Good records are:

  • accurate
  • timely
  • respectful
  • evidence-led
  • analytical
  • clear about legal basis
  • clear about the person's voice
  • clear about disagreement
  • clear about risk and protective factors
  • clear about decisions and review

Recording Formula

What happened?
Who said what?
What did I observe?
What evidence supports this?
What does the person want?
What law/policy applies?
What options were considered?
What decision was made?
Why is it proportionate?
What happens next?

Language to Avoid

Avoid:

  • non-compliant
  • attention-seeking
  • manipulative
  • difficult family
  • refusing to engage
  • lifestyle choice
  • bed blocker
  • failed placement
  • challenging behaviour without context

Use:

  • declined
  • distressed
  • disagreed
  • unable to attend
  • communication of unmet need
  • pattern of self-neglect
  • family disputes the plan
  • placement is no longer meeting needs

Defensible Decision-Making

A decision is more defensible when it shows:

  • relevant law and policy were considered
  • relevant information was gathered
  • the person was involved
  • capacity and consent were addressed
  • risks and benefits were balanced
  • less restrictive options were considered
  • discrimination and human rights were considered
  • reasons were recorded
  • review or contingency was set

Common Pitfalls

  • Recording professional opinion as fact.
  • Omitting the person's own words.
  • Describing behaviour without context.
  • Using vague risk phrases.
  • Copying forward old assessments.
  • Not explaining why options were rejected.
  • Forgetting equality and communication needs.
  • Recording "family agreed" without saying who, what authority they had, and whether the person agreed.

Welsh language and participation

Make an active offer of Welsh-language support and record how the preference will be met in assessment, meetings, plans and review. Check the organisation's Welsh language standards and arrangements. Language can affect trust, emotional expression and the reliability of an assessment; it is not simply a document-translation task.

Information sharing in Wales

Identify the purpose, lawful basis, people who need the information and the minimum relevant information. Consent is not always required to share safeguarding information, but there must be a lawful justification. Record the decision to share or not share and explain it to the person where appropriate and safe. Seek information-governance advice where the position is unclear. The ICO flags that its guidance is being reviewed following the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025; check updates for live decisions.

Practice exercise

Fictional case: an adult asks for Welsh-language meetings but the invitation and proposed support plan are in English. Their relative answers every question. Write three changes that would enable direct participation. Then record the difference between what the adult said, what the relative reported and your own analysis. Identify a review date and who will confirm that the communication changes worked.

Further local practice detail

The Human Rights Act 1998, Equality Act 2010, applicable data-protection law and Welsh-language duties engage different parts of a decision. More than just words promotes the Active Offer of Welsh-language care, recognising language as part of safe and effective support.

Distinguish a person’s language need and the organisation’s applicable Welsh-language standards from a general translation preference. Record how communication affects participation, consent, assessment and the reliability of evidence. A completed English form is not proof that the person could take part effectively.

Decision pathway

  • Ask and record language and communication needs and make the Active Offer practical.
  • Identify the actual equality, human-rights and Welsh-language duties of the service.
  • Check the purpose and lawful basis for information sharing, with appropriate limits.
  • Explain access, correction, complaints and the relevant rights or language challenge route.

Challenge and review in this pathway

A Welsh-language standards concern, a privacy complaint and a challenge to the care decision may require different bodies. Explain the organisation’s procedure and relevant external route. Address immediate access barriers while a complaint is considered.

Additional worked case

Fictional case: A Welsh-speaking adult appears confused during an English assessment but communicates clearly when suitable Welsh-language support is arranged.

Revisit the evidence and any capacity or eligibility conclusions affected by the communication barrier. Record the support required for future contacts and the person’s account, rather than retaining an unsupported label of confusion.

Common error to avoid: Treating language access as cosmetic or using a communication barrier as evidence of incapacity.
