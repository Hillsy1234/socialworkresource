03 - Mental Capacity Act 2005

Purpose

The Mental Capacity Act 2005 applies in England and Wales to people aged 16 and over. It provides the framework for supporting decision-making, assessing capacity, making best interests decisions, recognising advance planning, and protecting people who cannot make specific decisions for themselves.

The Five Principles

  1. Presume capacity unless it is established that the person lacks capacity.
  2. Take all practicable steps to support the person to decide.
  3. Do not treat a person as lacking capacity merely because they make an unwise decision.
  4. Any act or decision for a person who lacks capacity must be in their best interests.
  5. Before acting, consider whether the aim can be achieved in a less restrictive way.

Key Rule

Capacity is:

  • decision-specific
  • time-specific
  • evidence-specific
  • support-dependent

Do not write "lacks capacity" without saying "for what decision" and "at what time".

The Two-Stage Test

Stage 1: Diagnostic element

Is there an impairment of, or disturbance in, the functioning of the mind or brain?

This may include dementia, delirium, learning disability, acquired brain injury, mental disorder, intoxication, unconsciousness, or temporary confusion.

Stage 2: Functional element

Because of that impairment or disturbance, is the person unable to:

  • understand the relevant information
  • retain it long enough to decide
  • use or weigh it as part of the decision
  • communicate the decision by any means

If the answer is yes, the person lacks capacity for that decision at that time.

Support Before Assessment

Before concluding incapacity, consider:

  • best time of day
  • pain, infection, delirium, medication, distress, hunger, fatigue
  • interpreter or communication aid
  • speech and language therapy
  • easy read, pictures, objects of reference
  • trusted person involvement
  • quieter environment
  • repeated short conversations
  • cultural or spiritual context
  • whether the decision can wait

Relevant Information

Define the decision clearly. Then identify the information the person needs to understand, usually:

  • what the decision is
  • available options
  • likely benefits and risks of each option
  • what may happen if no decision is made
  • practical consequences
  • who may be affected

Keep the information no more complex than the decision requires.

Unwise Decisions

A person can understand and weigh information and still choose something others dislike. That is not incapacity.

Look at the decision-making process, not whether the decision seems sensible.

Executive Functioning

Some people can repeat information but cannot use it in real life. This may arise with brain injury, dementia, mental disorder, addiction, coercion, trauma, or learning disability.

Evidence may include:

  • repeated inability to carry out a stated plan
  • inability to adapt when risks occur
  • concrete examples from daily life
  • mismatch between verbal account and observed behaviour
  • professional evidence from OT, psychology, psychiatry, nursing, or support workers

Be careful: poor follow-through is not automatically incapacity. Analyse why it happens.

Best Interests

If the person lacks capacity for the decision, the decision-maker must act in best interests.

Consider:

  • the person's past and present wishes and feelings
  • beliefs and values
  • written statements
  • views of family, friends, carers, attorneys, deputies, advocates
  • whether capacity may return
  • all relevant circumstances
  • less restrictive options
  • risks and benefits
  • the person's involvement as far as possible

Best interests is not "what professionals prefer". It is a structured rights-based decision.

IMCA

An Independent Mental Capacity Advocate may be required in serious medical treatment or accommodation decisions where the person lacks capacity and has no appropriate person to consult.

IMCA may also be relevant in:

  • care reviews
  • safeguarding adults cases
  • DoLS processes

Check the statutory IMCA duty or power as well as local referral arrangements. Welsh independent professional advocacy under the social services framework is a separate route; identify which support is required.

Advance Planning

Look for:

  • Lasting Power of Attorney for health and welfare
  • Lasting Power of Attorney for property and financial affairs
  • Court-appointed deputy
  • Advance Decision to Refuse Treatment
  • advance statement of wishes and feelings
  • DNACPR or treatment escalation plan

Check validity, scope, and whether the attorney/deputy has authority for the specific decision.

MCA and Restraint

Restrictive intervention may be protected under the MCA only when:

  • the person lacks capacity for the relevant matter
  • the act is in best interests
  • restraint is necessary to prevent harm
  • restraint is proportionate to likelihood and seriousness of harm

If restrictions may amount to deprivation of liberty, consider DoLS or Court authorisation.

MCA and Mental Health Act

The relationship between the MCA, DoLS and MHA depends on the setting, purpose, consent or objection and the statutory eligibility rules. MCA best interests alone is not an authority to deprive someone of liberty. Where compulsory mental health assessment or treatment is being considered, involve the AMHP and treating team and establish the lawful route; do not assume that either framework automatically takes priority.

For a person detained under the Mental Health Act, MCA may still apply to decisions outside treatment for mental disorder, such as physical healthcare, accommodation after discharge, finances, or contact.

Common Pitfalls

  • Starting from risk rather than presumption of capacity.
  • Missing communication support.
  • Assessing global capacity instead of one decision.
  • Treating refusal as incapacity.
  • Treating diagnosis as incapacity.
  • Treating family concern as proof.
  • Writing best interests without options analysis.
  • Forgetting less restrictive alternatives.

Wales exercise

Fictional case: Sian speaks Welsh, is tired after hospital treatment and declines a proposed move. Specify the accommodation decision and relevant information. Plan a conversation in her preferred language at a better time, with accessible options. Record her explanation before drawing a conclusion. If capacity is absent, identify the decision-maker, any attorney or deputy and the available alternatives. A best interests record does not itself authorise deprivation of liberty.

Further local practice detail

The Mental Capacity Act 2005 applies to the specific decision and time. The person must be unable to decide because of an impairment or disturbance of mind or brain; inability concerns understanding, retaining, using or weighing relevant information, or communicating the decision. Support must be tried and an unwise choice is not itself incapacity.

An attorney, deputy, advance decision and a professional acting under the Act have different roles and limits. A best-interests decision should preserve participation and consider wishes, feelings, beliefs, values and less restrictive options. It does not itself answer every deprivation-of-liberty or mental-health authority question.

Decision pathway

  • Specify the decision and record support and evidence against the relevant abilities.
  • Explain the causal connection to impairment if concluding that capacity is lacking.
  • Check any attorney, deputy or advance decision and the scope of its authority.
  • Record best-interests reasoning, consultation, disagreement and the appropriate challenge route.

Challenge and review in this pathway

The Court of Protection deals with relevant disputes and decisions. Consider statutory advocacy requirements and specialist advice where serious disagreement or liberty issues arise. A relative’s agreement should not substitute for the required legal analysis.

Additional worked case

Fictional case: An adult refuses a proposed care arrangement after explaining its benefits and drawbacks, while relatives consider the choice too risky.

Explore the reasoning and support needs without treating disagreement as incapacity. If capacity is lacking, identify the exact authority and best-interests process. Keep the person’s wishes and any liberty issue separately recorded.

Common error to avoid: Treating professional disagreement, a diagnosis or a risk score as the capacity test.
