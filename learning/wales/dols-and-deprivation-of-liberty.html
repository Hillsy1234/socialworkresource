DoLS and Deprivation of Liberty

Learning outcomes

Describe restrictive arrangements accurately, distinguish mental capacity from the Article 5 consent question, and identify when specialist advice and an authorisation route need consideration.

The June 2026 change

On 2 June 2026 the Supreme Court departed from the approach in Cheshire West. The assessment requires attention to the person's actual situation and valid consent for Article 5 purposes; the former two-part acid test should not be used as the sole answer. Lack of domestic-law capacity does not, by itself, settle the distinct consent question. Mere acquiescence is not enough. Read the Supreme Court summary and linked judgment.

Care Inspectorate Wales said the judgment took effect immediately, existing arrangements should be reviewed, and legal advice sought where changes may be needed. MCA duties remain. Its statement is dated 15 June 2026; check for subsequent directions in your organisation. Welsh regulator statement.

Record the arrangements before deciding the route

Describe where the person lives, daily routines, supervision, locked doors, limits on contact or movement, medication-related restrictions, staff responses to a request to leave, duration and cumulative impact. Record what actually happens, not merely the care-plan wording. Identify which arrangements the person understands and actively wants, and any signs of distress, resistance or change. Consider their individual communication style and fluctuation over time.

Do not turn calm presentation, an absence of verbal objection or relatives' approval into evidence of consent. Equally, do not infer every necessary care arrangement is automatically a deprivation of liberty. Keep the legal analysis and the evidence supporting it explicit.

Settings and authorisation

Where the legal criteria are met, the MCA DoLS framework concerns adults aged 18 or over in hospitals and care homes. Community arrangements may require Court of Protection consideration; children and young people require careful advice about the applicable legal route. DoLS is not a general permission for treatment or every restriction. Use the Welsh guidance and forms with current specialist advice. Do not cancel or ignore existing safeguards solely because the law has changed.

Least restriction, representation and review

Ask what each restriction is intended to achieve and whether a less restrictive workable option exists. Consider changes to staffing, environment, communication, activities and support before assuming the present arrangement is inevitable. Record why an option was rejected and whose view informed that decision.

Check the person's access to representation, advocacy, information and challenge. Distinguish the care plan, any authorisation and its conditions, capacity decisions and best-interests decisions. Allocate responsibility for expiry dates, changed circumstances and objections; an authorisation is not the end of review.

Worked learning example

Fictional example: Carys lives in a care home with keypad exits. Staff say she is content, but she repeatedly packs a bag and asks for her old neighbour. Explore what this means to Carys, how staff respond and what support could clarify her wishes. Record actual restrictions and the evidence about understanding and consent. Discuss the legal analysis with the responsible specialist rather than relying on “she never tries the door”.

Supervision task

Complete the liberty-arrangements prompt using the fictional example. Separate known facts, communication observations, less restrictive options, capacity questions, the Article 5 question and the advice needed. The prompt supports analysis; it does not calculate or certify a legal outcome.

Further local practice detail

The Supreme Court’s June 2026 judgment requires the liberty analysis to distinguish the Article 5 consent question from domestic decision-making capacity. The old two-part checklist should not be used as the sole answer. Mere acquiescence does not establish valid consent.

Where required, the hospital or care-home DoLS route and community Court of Protection arrangements have different scope. The actual restrictions, the person’s understanding and wishes, the authorisation and the care or treatment decisions should remain separate in the record.

Decision pathway

  • Record the actual setting, restrictions, duration and response to a wish to leave.
  • Use current judgment and Welsh guidance with specialist advice on the applicable legal test.
  • Check the correct authorisation route, representation, conditions and review.
  • Explore less restrictive alternatives and act on objections or changed circumstances.

Challenge and review in this pathway

Explain the representative, advocacy and court challenge arrangements that apply. An existing authorisation should be reviewed through the responsible process; do not simply remove safeguards on the basis of a changed summary or training slide.

Additional worked case

Fictional case: A resident is quiet during meetings but repeatedly packs a bag and asks staff to take them home.

Use appropriate communication to understand the request and record how staff respond. Review actual restrictions and consent evidence with the responsible specialist. Calm presentation does not settle the legal question or remove the need to support a challenge.

Common error to avoid: Equating silence with consent or treating lack of capacity as the complete liberty analysis.
