Professional Foundations

Professional Foundations
Ontario practice guide practice guide
Learning prompt, not an official statutory form. Use fictional or anonymised information.

Local framework:
The Ontario College of Social Workers and Social Service Workers (OCSWSSW) regulates two professions with distinct scopes. Its Code of Ethics and Standards of Practice address relationships, competence, responsibilities, records, confidentiality, fees, communication and boundaries. Use the current standards alongside the legislation applying to the task.

Registration does not make every registrant a designated capacity assessor or confer every controlled-act or statutory power. Establish the role, competence and additional requirements before undertaking specialist work. Identify whether the employer is a health-information custodian, a public body or another organisation because that affects records and disclosure duties.

Local pathway:
Confirm registration, professional scope and any additional authority required.
Explain the service, purpose of assessment, informed consent and confidentiality limits.
Use supervision to examine bias, cultural safety and the effect of poverty or institutional barriers.
Connect continuing competence to changes in practice and the current College programme.

Recording prompts:
1. Your role and the legal or professional framework
Notes:

2. The person’s outcomes and communication preferences
Notes:

3. Evidence, competing explanations and missing perspectives
Notes:

4. Power, boundaries and any conflict of interest
Notes:

5. Agreed action, supervision, feedback and review
Notes:

Challenge and review:
A complaint to the College concerns professional conduct or practice. A funding appeal, health-service complaint or privacy complaint has a different route. Clarify which decision the person wants changed and who can address it.

Social Care and Support

Social Care and Support
Ontario practice guide practice guide
Learning prompt, not an official statutory form. Use fictional or anonymised information.

Local framework:
Ontario Health atHome coordinates important home-care and long-term-care placement functions. Distinguish home-care eligibility, the amount or type of service, placement eligibility and a provider’s delivery. Income support, developmental services, housing and privately arranged care have separate rules.

Ontario Health atHome identifies appealable matters including eligibility, service amounts, exclusion or discontinuation of services and long-term-care admission eligibility. Discussion with a coordinator, management review and an external appeal are not the same step. Obtain the decision and the appropriate route in writing.

Local pathway:
Identify the actual programme and responsible assessment or funding body.
Document daily needs, personal goals, informal support and the effect of service gaps.
Separate the eligibility decision, care-plan allocation, any charges and the start of provision.
Request reasons and track the relevant complaint, formal review or appeal for the disputed decision.

Recording prompts:
1. Daily-life evidence, fluctuation and desired outcomes
Notes:

2. The service, eligibility test and decision-maker
Notes:

3. Carer willingness and separately identified needs
Notes:

4. Funding, charges, decisions and reasons
Notes:

5. Start dates, unmet needs, contingency and challenge route
Notes:

Challenge and review:
Ontario Health atHome’s feedback process begins with the care coordinator and a patient-services manager and includes formal review for unresolved issues. Certain decisions may be appealed to the Health Services Appeal and Review Board. Check the notice and time limits; a provider-quality complaint is a separate issue.

Decision-Making and Capacity

Decision-Making and Capacity
Ontario practice guide practice guide
Learning prompt, not an official statutory form. Use fictional or anonymised information.

Local framework:
The Health Care Consent Act 1996 (HCCA) distinguishes treatment, admission to a care facility and personal-assistance decisions. Its capacity test asks whether the person understands relevant information and appreciates reasonably foreseeable consequences of deciding or not deciding. The Substitute Decisions Act 1992 separately addresses property and personal-care arrangements.

For HCCA substitute consent, apply the statutory hierarchy and eligibility requirements. Applicable prior capable wishes matter; where no applicable wish governs, the statutory best-interests framework applies. A power of attorney must be checked for scope and operation rather than assumed to cover every decision.

Local pathway:
Specify the decision and identify which Act and decision-maker apply.
Support understanding and assess the relevant understanding-and-appreciation abilities.
Check the substitute hierarchy, eligibility and any appointment before seeking a decision.
Identify applicable prior capable wishes and use the statutory decision standard where substitute consent is required.

Recording prompts:
1. Decision, options and time at which it is needed
Notes:

2. Communication support and observed responses
Notes:

3. Applicable legal test and evidence for each element
Notes:

4. Representative’s document, scope and decision standard
Notes:

5. Wishes, disagreement, review and any urgent action
Notes:

Challenge and review:
The Consent and Capacity Board has review and application functions under the HCCA and other legislation. Rights advice and procedural safeguards depend on the decision and setting. An SDA property assessment should not be substituted for a treating practitioner’s HCCA determination.

Sources:
https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/96h02

Restrictions and Liberty

Restrictions and Liberty
Ontario practice guide practice guide
Learning prompt, not an official statutory form. Use fictional or anonymised information.

Local framework:
Admission to a care facility, consent to treatment and authority to restrain or confine are distinct questions. The HCCA, Mental Health Act and setting-specific legislation such as the Fixing Long-Term Care Act 2021 may be relevant. Read the provision and its commencement note: an enacted future amendment is not an operative confinement power.

Long-term-care restraint requirements and mental-health detention procedures should not be treated as one system. A substitute’s agreement to admission does not by itself explain every locked-door arrangement or restraint used after admission.

Local pathway:
Identify the actual restriction, setting, legal status and the person’s wishes.
Separate admission consent from the specific authority for restraint or detention.
Check the current setting-specific conditions, documentation, monitoring and any emergency exception.
Arrange less restrictive support, rights information and the relevant Board, court or complaints route.

Recording prompts:
1. Each restriction, setting, duration and actual effect
Notes:

2. The person’s wishes, understanding and objections
Notes:

3. Legal authority, decision-maker, limits and conditions
Notes:

4. Alternatives tried and the evidence about necessity
Notes:

5. Representation, review date, expiry and challenge
Notes:

Challenge and review:
The person’s challenge route depends on the measure: a Consent and Capacity Board issue, a mental-health detention review or a long-term-care complaint may address different questions. Obtain advice about disputed confinement and preserve the person’s access to representation.

Mental Health

Mental Health
Ontario practice guide practice guide
Learning prompt, not an official statutory form. Use fictional or anonymised information.

Local framework:
Ontario’s Mental Health Act provides assessment and involuntary-admission routes. A Form 1 permits a psychiatric assessment period of up to 72 hours when its criteria are met; it is not itself indefinite detention or automatic authority for every treatment. Subsequent involuntary status requires the applicable certification and safeguards.

The Act has different routes and criteria, including provisions relating to harm or serious impairment and specific deterioration circumstances. Apply the full relevant provision rather than reducing every route to a single danger test. Treatment consent remains a separate HCCA question.

Local pathway:
Identify the exact assessment or admission authority and the evidence supporting its criteria.
Record the start, expiry and required next decision for the actual legal status.
Arrange the applicable notices, rights advice and access to legal representation or Board review.
Assess treatment consent separately and coordinate a real discharge and follow-up plan.

Recording prompts:
1. Mental and physical health evidence and the person’s account
Notes:

2. The local criteria and voluntary alternatives considered
Notes:

3. Separate authority for transport, admission and treatment
Notes:

4. Required notices, advocacy, representation and review
Notes:

5. Continuity, discharge responsibilities and crisis plan
Notes:

Challenge and review:
The Consent and Capacity Board reviews relevant involuntary-status and capacity matters. Rights advice is important when statutory notices or findings trigger it. A voluntary patient’s wish to leave requires the appropriate legal response; staff should not describe the person as detained without identifying a lawful basis.

Adult Safeguarding

Adult Safeguarding
Ontario practice guide practice guide
Learning prompt, not an official statutory form. Use fictional or anonymised information.

Local framework:
Ontario has setting-specific adult-protection and reporting rules. The Fixing Long-Term Care Act 2021 and Retirement Homes Act 2010 contain reporting obligations for specified suspected harms, with different recipients and statutory exceptions. Community elder-abuse concerns require a different analysis; do not assume a single universal adult-reporting duty.

For long-term-care matters, identify the Ministry’s reporting route; retirement-home matters involve the Retirement Homes Regulatory Authority (RHRA). Police, healthcare, the Public Guardian and Trustee or other supports may also be relevant depending on the facts and authority involved.

Local pathway:
Identify the adult’s setting and the specific suspected abuse, neglect, unlawful conduct or financial misuse.
Apply the reporting threshold and recipient under the relevant Act, including any applicable exception.
Arrange immediate safety and safe contact while considering consent, coercion and information sharing.
Confirm receipt, responsibility and ongoing practical support rather than treating the report as the end of the response.

Recording prompts:
1. Concern, source, chronology and the adult’s own account
Notes:

2. Immediate safety and a safe communication method
Notes:

3. The local reporting threshold, recipient and timing
Notes:

4. Consent, coercion and information-sharing basis
Notes:

5. Accepted responsibilities, outcomes and follow-up
Notes:

Challenge and review:
Explain the relevant Ministry, RHRA, police or service complaint route. A reporting obligation does not itself authorise a worker to take over the adult’s finances or accommodation. Capacity and representative-authority questions require their own framework.

Children, Families and Transitions

Children, Families and Transitions
Ontario practice guide practice guide
Learning prompt, not an official statutory form. Use fictional or anonymised information.

Local framework:
The Child, Youth and Family Services Act 2017 (CYFSA) includes a direct and ongoing duty to report specified protection concerns to a children’s aid society where there are reasonable grounds to suspect them. A person with the duty must report directly rather than rely on a manager or colleague.

The mandatory duty in this provision does not apply in the same way to 16- and 17-year-olds, although concerns about them may be reported and services may be available. Age and the actual statutory ground matter. Indigenous child and family service jurisdiction and applicable community laws also need checking where relevant.

Local pathway:
Identify the child’s age, concern and reasonable grounds under the CYFSA.
Make the required direct report to the appropriate society and further reports when new grounds arise.
Record the child’s words and immediate safety needs without conducting an unnecessary parallel investigation.
Coordinate family support and participation, identifying any applicable Indigenous service authority and legal framework.

Recording prompts:
1. Child’s account, communication and daily-life evidence
Notes:

2. Harm, strengths, needs and alternative explanations
Notes:

3. Reporting duty, recipient and action taken
Notes:

4. Family participation, culture and legal authority
Notes:

5. Named plan, education/health links and transition dates
Notes:

Challenge and review:
Explain the society’s role, support and applicable court or review processes. Confidentiality has statutory limits where reporting is required. A report about an older teenager should not be dismissed simply because the same mandatory provision does not apply.

Rights, Culture and Recording

Rights, Culture and Recording
Ontario practice guide practice guide
Learning prompt, not an official statutory form. Use fictional or anonymised information.

Local framework:
The Ontario Human Rights Code protects against discrimination in covered areas and requires accommodation within the applicable legal framework. The Canadian Charter has its own scope concerning government action. Do not assume the same rights mechanism applies to every private and public service.

The Personal Health Information Protection Act 2004 (PHIPA) applies according to the role of the custodian, agent and information. Other organisations may be governed by different privacy laws. College confidentiality and recording standards operate alongside those laws, not in place of them.

Local pathway:
Identify the service decision, relevant protected ground and accommodation required.
Check whether PHIPA or another privacy regime governs the organisation and proposed disclosure.
Separate consent to service, consent to treatment and consent or other authority for information sharing.
Record evidence and disagreement and explain access, correction, privacy complaints and the relevant rights remedy.

Recording prompts:
1. Decision, affected rights and responsible organisation
Notes:

2. Accessibility, culture and participation arrangements
Notes:

3. Disclosure purpose, legal basis, recipient and limits
Notes:

4. Evidence, alternatives, reasons and disagreement
Notes:

5. Access, correction, complaints and review route
Notes:

Challenge and review:
The Information and Privacy Commissioner of Ontario addresses privacy matters within its jurisdiction; the Human Rights Tribunal addresses applicable Code claims. A College complaint has a different purpose. Help the person seek the remedy relevant to the disputed act and obtain timely advice.
