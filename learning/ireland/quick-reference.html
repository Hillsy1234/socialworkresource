Professional Foundations — Ireland

CORU’s Social Workers Registration Board Code of Professional Conduct and Ethics is the professional anchor. Use the social-worker Code, rather than assuming that material for another CORU profession sets identical obligations. Registration, competence, confidentiality, professional boundaries and continuing learning need to be considered alongside the statutory function being performed.

HSE services, Tusla, local authorities and voluntary providers have different remits. Establish whether the task concerns adult health and support, child protection, housing or another service, and identify who holds the decision-making responsibility.

  • Confirm your professional role, competence and any specialist authority needed.
  • Explain the purpose of involvement and how the person can influence assessment and planning.
  • Separate the person’s wishes and evidence from organisational assumptions about risk or resources.
  • Use the current CORU CPD guidance to turn supervision and feedback into demonstrable learning.

A service complaint and a CORU fitness-to-practise concern address different issues. Help the person find the route relevant to the outcome they want, including the responsible organisation’s complaints and review processes.

  • CORU Social Workers Registration Board bye-laws
  • CORU CPD for social workers

Social Care and Support — Ireland

Map the actual HSE, disability, housing, income-support or nursing-home scheme. HSE Home Support for older people provides assistance with everyday tasks following assessment; it is generally directed to people aged 65 or over, with some younger people also eligible. The published service is free, but an assessment and funding decision must still be followed through to delivery.

Home Support, disability services and the Nursing Homes Support Scheme are distinct routes. Do not describe one approval as covering all care settings or assume that the rules for nursing-home financial support apply to home support.

  • Identify daily-living needs and which HSE service or other body is responsible for the requested support.
  • Use the relevant application and needs-assessment process, including discharge coordination if the person is in hospital.
  • Separate the allocation of support from service availability, start dates and any other scheme’s financial assessment.
  • Include family willingness, respite needs and a contingency if provision is delayed.

Ask the responsible service for the assessment outcome, reasons and review or complaint route. HSE’s Home Support guidance explains applications and local contacts. Where more than one scheme is involved, track each decision and its challenge route separately.

  • Applying for Home Support

Decision-Making and Capacity — Ireland

The Assisted Decision-Making (Capacity) Act 2015, as amended, uses a functional, decision- and time-specific approach. Presume capacity, provide support and consider understanding, retention, use or weighing of relevant information, and communication. The person’s will and preferences are central to the statutory approach.

Decision-making assistants, co-decision-makers and court-appointed decision-making representatives have different functions. Enduring powers of attorney and advance healthcare directives require their own checks. The Decision Support Service (DSS) registers or supervises relevant arrangements and provides Codes; it does not itself become the person’s decision-maker.

  • State the precise decision and provide accessible information and communication support.
  • Use the DSS capacity-assessment Code and record evidence against the relevant functional abilities.
  • Read the actual agreement, registration or court order and verify what the supporter or representative can do.
  • Check whether an applicable advance healthcare directive or other arrangement governs the healthcare decision.

Use the appropriate DSS complaint or court route for concerns about arrangements or representatives. A disagreement about treatment may require clinical and legal advice under the HSE Consent Policy. Distinguish support to decide from authority to decide for someone.

  • DSS capacity and decision-support Code
  • Assisted Decision-Making (Capacity) Act 2015
  • DSS guidance materials

Restrictions and Liberty — Ireland

Constitutional liberty and the applicable consent and mental-health law must be considered where a person is prevented from leaving a care setting. Incapacity, good intentions and relatives’ agreement do not supply general authority to detain.

The HSE National Consent Policy addresses deprivation of liberty and the need for legal advice and appropriate court involvement. Distinguish an ordinary care arrangement from detention, and distinguish a decision-support arrangement from authority for confinement. Do not start a new wardship process as though it remained the ordinary route after the decision-support reforms.

  • Describe what happens if the person asks to leave and record their will and preferences.
  • Check capacity for the relevant decision without treating incapacity as a detention authorisation.
  • Use the current HSE Consent Policy and seek urgent legal advice where the person is held contrary to their wishes.
  • Identify the precise court or mental-health authority relied on, its limits and a plan to reduce restrictions.

Arrange independent support and access to legal advice where liberty is disputed. Court involvement and review must address the actual arrangement; a family meeting cannot confer the necessary power. Immediate action should not become indefinite confinement without review.

  • HSE National Consent Policy

Mental Health — Ireland

The Mental Health Act 2026 was signed into law on 7 May 2026. Section 1 provides for commencement by ministerial order, potentially on different dates for different provisions. Enactment must not be treated as evidence that all new admission, treatment or review rules have replaced the 2001 framework.

For a real case, identify the operative provisions and current Mental Health Commission (MHC) guidance. The existing 2001 framework has separate application, medical recommendation and consultant-psychiatrist admission stages, with independent tribunal safeguards. Explain the route actually in force for the person rather than mixing stages from old and new legislation.

  • Check the commencement record and current MHC operational guidance before choosing forms, criteria or time limits.
  • Identify voluntary alternatives and the purpose of the proposed assessment, admission or treatment.
  • Verify the authorised professionals and evidence required by the operative route.
  • Arrange the applicable legal representation, independent review and treatment safeguards, and record which legal version was used.

The person needs an accessible explanation of their status, legal support and the applicable tribunal or court process. A mental-health admission does not resolve every physical-health consent or accommodation decision. Check each intervention separately.

  • Mental Health Act 2026, commencement provision
  • Department of Health enactment announcement
  • Mental Health Act 2001
  • Mental Health Commission

Adult Safeguarding — Ireland

The Department of Health published the National Policy Framework for Adult Safeguarding in the Health and Social Care Sector in December 2025, with the publication page updated in February 2026. Read it alongside the operative HSE safeguarding procedures and the arrangements in the actual service. An older policy link alone does not describe the whole current policy picture.

Separate a safeguarding concern, a criminal matter, a provider-quality issue and a decision-support problem. HSE safeguarding services, An Garda Síochána, HIQA and the DSS have different remits. A safeguarding referral does not automatically create authority to override the adult’s decision.

  • Identify the concern and safe contact arrangements, including coercion and dependency.
  • Use the service’s current safeguarding procedure and responsible HSE safeguarding route.
  • Refer criminal or urgent safety issues to the appropriate Garda or emergency response where needed.
  • Check consent, decision support and any legal authority separately from the protection plan.

Ask about the adult’s desired outcomes and offer independent advocacy where appropriate. Explain the relevant service complaint, regulatory or DSS route. Record the policy version and whether a new national framework has been implemented through the service’s operative procedure.

  • Department of Health adult safeguarding updates

Children, Families and Transitions — Ireland

The Children First Act 2015 creates reporting obligations for mandated persons; the Child Care Act 1991 supplies important child-welfare and protection functions. Tusla’s Children First guidance distinguishes the statutory harm threshold from other reasonable welfare concerns that should still be reported through the appropriate route.

For mandated reporting, harm includes assault, ill-treatment or neglect seriously affecting or likely seriously to affect health, development or welfare, and sexual abuse. Apply the guidance for the category and circumstances, including its specific provisions on disclosures. Do not require proof before considering reasonable grounds.

  • Establish whether you are a mandated person and whether the concern meets the statutory reporting threshold.
  • Report to Tusla through the correct process; follow emergency arrangements where a child is in immediate danger.
  • Record the child’s words, the facts supporting the concern and what was reported.
  • Coordinate family support, education and health, keeping any court or protection authority distinct.

Explain the assessment or protection process and how the child and family can participate and obtain support or legal advice. A concern below the mandated threshold can still warrant a welfare report; the threshold is not a reason to ignore other needs.

  • Tusla mandated reporting thresholds
  • Children First guidance and legislation
  • Children First Act 2015

Rights, Culture and Recording — Ireland

The Equal Status Acts, constitutional rights, the European Convention on Human Rights Act 2003 and the Irish Human Rights and Equality Commission Act 2014 form part of the rights map. The public-sector equality and human-rights duty requires public bodies to integrate equality and rights into their functions.

GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018 govern personal-data processing alongside professional confidentiality and sector-specific duties. Identify the controller, purpose, lawful basis and any additional condition for sensitive data. A request from another agency does not itself establish permission to send a full record.

  • Identify the right or equality duty engaged by the actual service decision.
  • Consider accessibility, discrimination and the person’s preferred cultural and communication support.
  • Document the legal basis and necessary scope of a disclosure, including consent or another applicable basis.
  • Explain access, correction and the relevant service, equality or data-protection complaint route.

IHREC provides public-sector duty guidance; the Data Protection Commission addresses data-protection matters. A service complaint or legal challenge may be needed for the underlying decision. Help the person identify the remedy and obtain advice within the applicable time limits.

  • Data Protection Act 2018
